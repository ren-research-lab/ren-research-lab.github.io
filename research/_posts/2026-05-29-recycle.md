Critical minerals are essential to batteries, EVs, semiconductors, and low-carbon technologies, yet their production remains constrained by complex ores, dilute waste streams, high-temperature processing, and chemical-intensive extraction and separation steps. Our group develops electrochemical approaches to recover and regenerate these materials from primary and secondary resources, including mining residues, industrial wastes, battery manufacturing scraps, and end-of-life energy materials.

Rather than treating mineral extraction as a purely thermal or chemical process, we view it as an interfacial electrochemical problem. Electricity can be used to tune redox potential, local pH, ion transport, and solid–liquid reactions inside a reactor, allowing valuable elements to be selectively dissolved, transported, separated, and re-precipitated. This approach creates opportunities to replace harsh reagents and high-temperature steps with modular electrochemical systems that can be powered by low-carbon electricity.

Our work builds from the concept of electrified resource recovery. In previous studies, related electrochemical reactor designs enabled calcium extraction from waste cement and regeneration of cement clinker precursors through coupled acid generation, calcium dissolution, and hydroxide precipitation. We are extending this principle toward critical mineral systems, where similar electrochemical control can be used to recover lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese, copper, rare earth elements, and other strategically important materials from chemically complex feedstocks.

By combining extractive metallurgy, electrochemical engineering, materials characterization, and process modeling, we aim to understand how metals move through solids, electrolytes, membranes, and interfaces. The long-term goal is to develop scalable electrochemical recycling platforms that reduce waste, lower emissions, and build more circular supply chains for clean energy materials.
